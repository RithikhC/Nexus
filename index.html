The limit is sent over the IEC 61851 control pilot — the standard signal every charge point already uses to tell a car its maximum current. It can ask for anything from 6 A (1.4 kW) up to 80 A; below 6 A a session has to pause rather than slow down further.

This sits above the controller, not inside it. Protection is automatic and never waits for anyone — if the load crosses the limit the controller acts that minute. The advisor works on a slower clock: it watches whole evenings, and when a pattern appears it puts ranked courses of action in front of a human. You choose one; nothing happens until you do.

This is how utilities already run it. Restoration functions in an ADMS are commissioned the same way — GE Vernova's D400-DA, in Operator Assisted Mode, “will first ask for confirmation from the operator before taking any action. If the operator does not agree with the recommended action, the D400-DA will calculate the next optimal scenario and propose that to the operator.” Oracle's network management system has a manual mode where “restoration solutions are automatically generated but must be manually executed by the operator.” Utilities typically start in this mode and only move to automatic once confidence is earned.

What carries each step, in a real network

  1. Measurement in. Transformer loading reaches the control centre over the usual telecontrol protocols — DNP3 (IEEE 1815) in North America, IEC 60870-5-104 in Europe and much of Asia — from an RTU or gateway, with IEC 61850 inside the substation itself.
  2. Where this would live. In the utility's ADMS, beside functions like FLISR, exchanging data with metering and other systems through the IEC 61968 Common Information Model. 61968 defines application-level message interfaces; it is a separate layer from the telecontrol protocols above, not an extension of them.
  3. The decision. Operator-assisted, as described above. Nothing is dispatched on the advisor's own authority.
  4. Command out. The approved limit reaches a charge point as an OCPP 1.6J / 2.0.1 charging profile, and the charge point applies it on the IEC 61851-1 control pilot — 6 A to 80 A, the whole range the standard can express.

The grid runs on everyone being different

A transformer is never built to supply every home at once, because people don't all do the same thing at the same time. The whole network is sized around that spread.

Here, that spread is already thinner

Dubai's household load is mostly air conditioning, and cooling is driven by one thing every home shares — the outdoor temperature. At 42 °C the whole street cools at once, before any car plugs in.

Charging breaks it further

A home charger pulls about as much as the rest of the house combined, and everyone plugs in when they get home. Now every home is doing the same thing at the same moment.

Nobody needs it all at once

A car home at 6pm and leaving at 8am has far longer than it needs. The controller spreads it, giving power first to whoever leaves soonest — though here the overnight valley is shallow, because the cooling never really stops.

About the numbers. This is a teaching model of one street, not a study of a real network. The pattern is the point; the exact figures follow from the assumptions set out in the paper. Nothing here is a measurement.
